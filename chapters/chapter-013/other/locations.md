# Chapter 013 — Locations

Places used in this chapter. Basin locations from earlier chapters are defined in
`chapter-012/other/locations.md` and `chapter-011/other/locations.md`.

## THE COUNTER, SECOND OFFICE

The Knot & Nail's counter as the basin's working table of record. From Chapter 013 Page 001 it holds:
the register (open, back pocket fat with the basin's paper), the lamp, Kessa's shut box, and a queue
that starts at the fourth bell. Rulings are entered in the Office's grammar, in the keeper's book,
with the claimant's mark. One table where a record gets made and nobody's uncle owns the table.

## THE SHUT HATCH

The Office's public service hatch across the square, shuttered with a knot-marked notice: *pending
the count.* The basin's only stamp is behind it. A handful of basin folk stand before it each
morning, unreadable, then turn and walk toward the stall — which is the entire trade policy of the
suspension, written in feet. Its board has since carried the amendment, the permit-dark, and the
endorsement demand — each sealed, each fetched across the square to be read aloud at the counter
(Pages 004–006). Page 007 added the first unsealed thing it ever displayed: the courtesy filing,
posted before the lamps, no wax, no name — and the street's readers knew at a glance that its
bareness was the message. Page 009: at dusk, the clerk himself — erased by his own office that
morning, plain coat, empty cord, no paper — standing in the queue of the basin's people before it,
asking for nothing. Entered of record. *Him, too.*

## THE COUNTER'S TABLE

The counter-top as the chapter leaves it (Page 010): the Office's sealed weekly delivery flat beside
the open register — machine wax and basin knot-marks in each other's reach, each paper under the
other's grammar now. One table where a record gets made and nobody's uncle owns the table; two books
at it, and the whole basin allowed to read.

## THE ALLEY ROOM

The school's room behind the door-frame knot — the room the lease covers and the inspection demand
targets (Pages 001–003). One door, one shuttered window, a bench, bedding for three, a fuel store,
the crate, and four entry-racks: the roll, whole. The room keeps its own books — possession by knot,
entry by knot — so when the Office's slate counts it (Page 003), the count comes back correct,
sealed, and empty of persons: a room where nobody lives, which is a room the Office cannot tax. The
three entered hands hold its tenancy, unnamed.

## THE FUEL-YARD GATE

The foreman's gate at the fuel-yard, with its knot-board (Page 004): where the Office's runner
delivered the amendment's own copy the same day the register read it — and where, one day later, the
runner delivered the register's sealed answer to the same gate (Page 005). The foreman read it twice,
honoured the certificate in view of his yard, and made the gate's rule out loud: paid against the
keeper's paper, stands till the count ends. The gate that chose which paper it trusts — and paid for
the choice.

## THE ENDORSEMENT WINDOW

The hinged frame built into the stall's shutter overnight (Page 006), after the clerk's endorsement
trap: paper presented here is endorsed here — witnessed true, entered of record, road intact,
answerable at this counter. Knot-marks burnt into the sill-board; the window's own little seal hangs
beside it on a cord; the keeper's name goes on every endorsement at its foot. First in line: the
claimant, whose certificate now states its own validity on its face. First morning's take: an old
debt acknowledged under a dead man's seal, and a hire-promise cord with no seal anywhere — the basin
has been holding paper no office would ever read. The window's price is knotted into the till-cord:
one candle a week, two till-hours a day, we witness, we do not mint. Page 008: the Office's sealed
notice claims its filings are received "as a courtesy" — and the clerk himself crosses the counter's
threshold at dusk to carry the fair terms back, under seal and his own thumb-mark.
