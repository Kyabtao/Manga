# THREADBORN — Chapter 09 — Page 007

**Chapter title:** *The Reading* / *पाठ*
**Arc:** II — The Mendery
**Sector:** Agnikhand — the Mendery, the cellar, the day of the blank
**Page type:** Decision page — 7 panels

> Beats: the register is certified, the deposit is written, and the keeper has three days to put a
> name in a blank at the top of an identification form — the hand the instrument read at the fourth
> hour past third bell.
>
> **This page:** what a name is, and what the school has instead of one.

---

## PANEL 1 — Medium, the blank (~13%)

**Camera:** The cellar, morning, the desk. On it: the register open at its first spread with entry one
reserved and darkening; the certification sheet drying; and the **identification form** on top, with
its blank at the head, and beside the blank a line for the *manner* of identification — a printer's
space for a name, a kind, and a year. The clerk's pen has been left on the desk, which is a form of
pressure.

**Image:** A blank the width of a name, and a room full of people who have made sure nobody ever had
one.

> **CAPTION (Ira's voice):**
> Three days, he said, from the bell, and it was the second morning, so I had two.
>
> **CAPTION:**
> The form wants a name in the box and it wants the name *read out* while the keeper writes, so that
> everybody in the room hears it. I know why. A name said out loud in a cellar with a clerk in it is a
> name that has been *entered.* That is the entire difference between a name and a person.

---

## PANEL 2 — Medium, Kessa's case (~14%)

**Camera:** Kessa, sitting, doing the arithmetic out loud and hating every word of it because she is
right. Between her fingers, the tally-thread; on the desk, the register. She does not look at the
blank.

**Image:** The appraiser arguing for the least-bad outcome, which is what she has been doing all her
life.

> **KESSA:**
> Write it.
>
> **IRA:**
> Kessa —
>
> **KESSA:**
> Write the name, child, and be quick about it, and let me tell you what you're buying. Right now your
> form has one blank in it and the Office has one piece of paper. If you write it, the Office has a
> name, and a name on a page is a handle, and handles get pulled. If you don't write it, the Office
> hasn't got a name *and doesn't need one* — because clause two says any person who knows may declare,
> and there are four hundred people on that pot-row who will be very glad to be helpful about somebody
> else's hand.
>
> **KESSA:**
> Forty years your mother kept no names in a book so that nobody could be helpful. You cannot hold
> that line with a blank on your own desk.

---

## PANEL 3 — Close, Rekhak's case (~14%)

**Camera:** Close: Rekhak at the cellar door, not in the room. He has not been invited and he is not
coming down the steps. His hand is on the door-frame, chain wound twice around the wrist, the dark
link thumb-side.

**Image:** The bought reader offering the only thing he still owns: a reading.

> **REKHAK:**
> I'll make you a case, keeper, since the whole basin heard me sell mine this morning.
>
> **REKHAK:**
> Write *unknown,* and refuse the identification, and clause four takes the stall, and then the Office
> has the keeping, the register, the cellar, the archives and the Book — and the register gets filled
> anyway, by a clerk, from up-terrace, one line at a time, for years, with no keeper in the room.
>
> **REKHAK:**
> Write the name and you've given them one hand. Refuse and you've given them the *desk.*

> **JADI (off-panel, from the counter above, flat):**
> Nobody's writing anything yet.

---

## PANEL 4 — Medium, the first pupil comes down (~15%)

**Camera:** Jadi comes down the cellar steps — the first time she has come down them since the register
arrived, and the panel must make the reader feel what that costs her, because on Chapter 009 Page 004
she watched this room from the stair and swore she would not come in. She stops at the bottom and
looks at the register, and *reads the drawing* of her own line the way she read line one.

**Image:** The woman the blank is for, walking into the room the blank is kept in, because the school's
rule is that the teacher tells her what she did.

> **JADI:**
> You said rule three.
>
> **IRA:**
> I said it.
>
> **JADI:**
> Then say it. Whatever you're going to put in that box, say it to me first, out loud, in front of
> these two, because if it's my name then I want to hear it said in a room where I'm standing up.
>
> **JADI:**
> *(and then, quieter, to the form)*
> I can't read a word of it. I know exactly what it says.

---

## PANEL 5 — Close, Ira's answer (~15%)

**Camera:** Close on the form. Ira's hand takes the pen, and then does not write a name — she writes
in the *manner* line first, which is the trick: the ordinance clause two says a hand shall be declared
and entered; it never once says a person shall be named. In art, the box for the name stays **empty**,
and the manner line fills with three short entries drawn as abstract strokes with one legible word:
**KIND.**

**Image:** The school answering an Office form in the school's own language.

> **IRA:**
> Clause two. *Every such hand shall be declared and entered in the Register.* It says entered. It
> does not say named.
>
> **IRA:**
> So I'm entering the hand the way this school has entered every hand for forty years. Kind. Year,
> where the year is the school's year and not the Office's. And the knot — her first knot, the one
> she tied the day she was enrolled, which is stitched down on page one of the Book and which is the
> only thing about her that has ever been written anywhere.
>
> **IRA:**
> No name in the box, Jadi. Not yours and not anybody's. *You told me: no mark under your line.*

> **CLERK:**
> *(small, worried, honest)*
> Keeper. I don't know if that's an entry. I've never seen one.
>
> **IRA:**
> Then write down that you've seen one now, and put the time on it.

---

## PANEL 6 — Large, the knot goes in (~16%)

**Camera:** Large panel, macro: the register's line two, and Ira's hand setting a **knot** into the
paper — not drawn, not written: a knot, seat of thread, pressed and drawn through, exactly as the Book
of the Hand has one for every pupil. Beside it, her entries: kind, school-year, and a stroke for the
seam. The register's line two now reads like a page of her mother's book, sitting inside the Office's
own instrument, in the Office's own cellar.

**Image:** The school's first pupil entered, at last, in the only kind of book the school ever trusted —
inside the kind of book it never did.

> **CAPTION (Ira's voice):**
> It took four minutes and it is the single most dangerous thing I have ever done to a piece of paper.
>
> **CAPTION:**
> Because a knot cannot be looked up. My mother built forty years of safety on that sentence. And I
> have just put a knot — *her* knot, the first one, the one stitched into the Book of the Hand —
> into an Office register that is kept in the same cellar as the Book of the Hand.

> **KESSA:**
> *(very quietly, looking at the knot)*
> Girl. You've just taught the Office to read us.

---

## PANEL 7 — Close, what it costs — CLOSING HOOK (~14%)

**Camera:** Close: the clerk, blotting the entry, reading it back as the procedure requires, and
getting to the box that is empty. His voice does not change. Behind him, at the stairs, the Inspector
has arrived — early, without announcement — and is standing where he can see the desk, saying nothing
at all.

**Image:** The Office accepting a lawful entry it cannot use, and beginning to think about how to use
it.

> **CLERK:**
> Keeping entered. One hand, declared. Kind — read. Year — read. Knot — *recorded.*
>
> **CLERK:**
> Name. *(beat)* Name — *none entered.*
>
> **INSPECTOR:**
> *(from the stairs, mild, to nobody)*
> No. Not none. *None* is a word we use when a hand is unknown.
>
> **INSPECTOR (last line):**
> What the keeper has entered is a *translation.* The Office has never had one of those before, and I
> am told we are very good at them.

> **CAPTION (Ira's voice, smallest):**
> And that is when I understood what I had done, standing in my own cellar with a pen in my hand.
>
> **CAPTION (smallest):**
> I hadn't protected anybody. I had *published* the language.

## Writing & art notes

- **This is the chapter's turn and it is Ira being clever in exactly the way Chapter 008 warned
  about.** Her move is lawful, it preserves the school's founding rule (no names in a book), and it
  is precisely the thing that makes the school legible to the Office. Do not let anyone on the page
  call it a triumph, and do not let Ira congratulate herself: Kessa names the cost, the Inspector
  confirms it, and Ira's own captions arrive late.
- **Do not write a name anywhere on this page.** The name box stays empty in art from Panel 1 to
  Panel 7. The only legible words in the whole page are **KIND** in the manner line and the clerk's
  recited phrase *none entered.*
- **The knot is drawn, not lettered.** A real knot in the paper, seat of thread, matching the Book of
  the Hand's knot style (Ch. 008 Page 003). A scrolling reader must connect it to Jadi's page in the
  Book without being told.
- **Jadi's entry into the cellar is the page's emotional spine.** She swore she would never willingly
  come down here. She comes down because the amended rule three obliges the teacher to tell her what
  she did, and Jadi wants it said *in a room where she is standing up.* Play this flatly; it is the
  first time the school's own rule is used as a demand on the school.
- **"You cannot hold that line with a blank on your own desk" is Kessa's whole worldview in one
  sentence.** She is not wrong. The page should leave the reader unable to say whether Ira's answer is
  better or worse than writing the name — that ambiguity is Chapter 010's fuel.
- **The Inspector's "we are very good at translations" is the hook.** No threat, no raised voice, and
  no further explanation on this page. Chapter 009 Pages 008–010 do the work: the pot-row filling its
  own lines, the Book of the Hand in the same cellar as the register, and the Office's first act of
  translation.
