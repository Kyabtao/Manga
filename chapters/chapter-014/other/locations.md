# Chapter 014 — Locations

Places used in this chapter. Basin locations from earlier chapters are defined in
`chapter-013/other/locations.md` and earlier.

## THE STALL IN SNOW

The Knot & Nail through its first winter: the window open in snowfall, papers kept dry inside coats,
hot cups set into the frame for the first in line, the oil-cord's three knots hanging by the till.
The counter that owes the basin an honest account of the machine — including the days the machine's
paper does not come.

## THE ARCHIVE WALL, MIDWINTER

The school's oldest rack at the year's turning: the lowest rod silver with age, the dense year-knot
at its head, Nandi's brass lens and the keeper's lamp on it together at midnight. Thirty-nine years
of the old school, counted in a grammar the Office's nineteen-year roll cannot read.

## NANDI'S BOOK

Small, thick, older than any Office roll: the reader's own count in the old grammar, no lettering —
where debtor lines live when the price of a reading is a line in somebody's count. From Page 002 it
holds the keeper's line: *Ira Sutar — took one reading, paid one line.* The second Office's founder,
a debtor in a record older than the machine.

## THE HATCH BOARD IN WINTER

The Office's public notice-board under snow, and the basin's morning weather-report of record:
whatever is posted here is fetched across the square and read aloud at the counter before the
machine's own hands arrive. From Page 003 it carries the season's confession — the permit-ledger
discontinued, the light folded into the fuel, *for the season* — and the basin moves on it together,
before anyone official arrives.

## THE STALL WINDOW, KEPT OPEN

The stall's open shutter as a stated policy from Page 004: same hours, same terms, same readings, all
winter, said from the sill so the whole queue hears it. After this page the open window is not
hospitality and not stubbornness — it is the counter's answer to a hold, kept open on purpose where an
unknown protector can watch the basin be careful with his gift.

## THE HATCH BOARD AT DUSK

The Office's public notice-board seen at the other end of the day: blue snow-light, the notice under a
crusted ridge of snow, the board's cords stiff with cold. From Page 004 it is also where the protector
works — a snow-heavy figure, no face, reading the season notice back by touch and re-tying two knots
on one public cord in machine grammar, leaving a glove-mark in the snow. The basin watches from the
stall's far window and does not move.

## THE SCHOOL'S SIDE ROOM, WINTER CLASS

The archive room with its table pushed to the wall and benches brought in: coats kept on, a short
practice cord in every lap, the brass lens set down unused because the lesson is about finish and not
thread. From Page 005 it is the basin's first schoolroom for machine grammar — nine women, one
teacher, the till at the door-frame taking a line a head. Cold, crowded, lit by one lamp and the
window: an institution, not a lecture hall.

## THE RECEIVED TRAY

The shallow tray at the stall's counter where delivered papers go face-down when they are entered but
not signed. From Page 005 it holds the Office's courtesy form: the first machine document the counter
has accepted without a line of its own on it. The tray is the physical shape of the register's third
grammar — *entered as received* — and it sits in the queue's sight, on purpose.

## THE TILL, OPEN

The stall's brass-bound till with its lid up — a sight the chapter has withheld until Page 006. Inside:
paper tallies bound in cord, a shallow tray of Office coin, Kessa's ash-ink pot. Opened that page as a
purse rather than a drawer: nine lines counted onto the school's book in one entering, the book set
between the till and the queue so the square can see one hand pay and not nine pockets. From this
page the till is the second Office's first public money.

## THE COUNTER'S BACK POCKET

The register's back pocket where Rekhak's folded paper has lived, unopened, since Page 001 — the
physical form of *protection of record*. From Page 006 it is also a place a man can put two fingers
in public without taking anything: the fold's edge visible at the pocket's corner, the paper never
drawn out. The counter's most private promise, tested in the open, at dusk, in front of the queue.

## THE WATCH SILL

The stall's window sill, snow on the wood, in the lamplight's spill — from Page 007 the place where an
unnamed hand can put something down at an arm's length and be read without being followed. It holds
two knots at dawn: the protector's, in the wrong hand's finish, and the counter's plain answer beside
it. The most exposed surface the second Office owns, and the only one it has ever used to answer a
letter.

## THE STALL'S DOOR, BARRED

The stall's doorway at night, with Kessa's arm across it and the slate in her other hand. From Page
007 it is where the counter's policy is enforced against its own people: the queue's young watch
wanted to follow the figure at the sill, and the till said no, and the door was shut. The night the
window's public promise became a thing the basin had to pay for.

## THE OFFICE'S POSTED HOURS

The knotted cord under the hatch notice (from Page 006), read from on the night watch: six shifts the
Office counts and one it does not — *the fuel-yards, at night.* From Page 007 the cord is the
counter's night text, read aloud twice an hour to an empty street. Not a notice any more; a lesson
the basin recites.

## THE COUNCIL STAIR, MIDDAY

The black basalt flights cut back and forth up to the Office's blank face, seen from the basin's foot
in midday snow with ash falling alongside the snow. From Page 008 it is the distance a counter's
question has to walk when the counter has no authority to send with it: one runner, one page, no
guard, no gate, no confrontation — the queue stopped at the stair's foot to watch him go, and nobody
following.

## THE DEMAND TABLE

The archive table with the lamp on it and the year's rods racked behind — from Page 008 the place the
second Office drafts its instruments: the demand's four knots tied by Nandi, the copy written by the
keeper, the wax and the seal brought by the till, the school's women practising the same tie on their
own cords. A drafting room that is also a classroom, because in this institution it is the same skill.

## THE STALL'S DOOR

The second Office's only entrance, opened a hand's width on a dusk-blue street in deep winter — from
Page 009 the place where its written protections come due. A snow-loaded figure stood outside it
without knocking; the bar was lifted from inside, in front of the queue, and the man came in. The door
is shut for the first time in the chapter on this page, because the night belongs to the door and not
to the window; it opens again at dawn, for the reading.

## THE CUSTODY RING

A plain cord laid in a loose ring around the folded sheet on the counter and deliberately left untied
(Page 009). The counter's whole statement about holding a dangerous paper without secrecy: the ring
marks what is held and who may open it — everyone, at dawn, at the window, aloud. Nothing is knotted,
because a knot would be a decision the basin has not been given.

## THE WINDOW AT DAWN

The stall's open window in grey first light, with the square standing in the snow before it in a
half-circle — not a queue, a room with no walls. From Page 010 the chapter's last setting and its most
public: the machine's own night-count read aloud here, clause by clause, to the quarter it fed, with
the counter's reading written on the sheet and the lamp out, because nothing the counter holds is read
in the dark.

## THE SILL, TWO KNOTS

The window sill where the protector's knot lay on Page 007 and the counter's answer beside it. The
chapter ends with the sill empty except for those two knots, exactly where they were left, and one
line of prints softening in the slush beyond the door — the whole record of an unnamed correspondence.
