# Chapter 014 — Glossary

New terms and usages introduced in this chapter. Chapter 013's terms are defined in
`chapter-013/other/glossary.md`.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **The first miss** | The weekly delivery's first late day (Page 001, snow): the terms running for ten weeks, then the count-house's satchel nowhere on the road. Entered of record without excuse — the register holds the day open until dusk, then the miss becomes the record, and the record does not care why. The basin discovers its new dependency by standing in it, in the cold. |
| **Entered unread** | The second protection of record (Page 001) — the first ever volunteered: Rekhak's paper, carried since before the counter existed, goes into the register's back pocket UNOPENED. The register protects the fold, not the contents — which is why it can be trusted with both. His thumb-mark goes into the wax beside the keeper's knot: the basin's grammar, chosen, not learned under duress. |
| **The year of the second chain** | The year-knot's reading (Page 001): the school's oldest rod was opened in a year counted as nineteen BEFORE the Office's Freedom — the machine's roll is nineteen years old; the school's archive is thirty-nine. Everything the Office ever counted, it counted inside a room that was already full. The knot is not a date, Nandi says. It is a direction. |
| **"Invoice it"** | The chapter's ladder run (Page 001): the un-invoiced year — the one the finale let turn unannounced — is now on the table, and the keeper's order of business is to enter its cost of record. The second Office's first winter job: count what was never counted, starting with the years. |
| **The debtor's line** | The keeper's entry on the school's books (Page 002): Nandi's own book — small, thick, older than any Office roll — takes Ira Sutar's line in the old grammar: *took one reading, paid one line*, settled forward until the bought count is paid. The old grammar asks what you owe before you sit down. The keeper's hand shook at the grammar, not the debt. |
| **Thirty-nine, unbroken** | The oldest rod, unrolled (Page 002): the year-knot at its head, then a season-knot for every season — thirty-nine years, no gap, no stolen stretch, no ruled-out stretch of white. The basin's actual record, kept in a grammar the Office cannot read. Theirs is nineteen and full of white spaces. Nandi keeps the count; Ira built a counter. Not the same work — the same direction. |
| **The new tag** | The thin delivery's cold hook (Page 002): the satchel came re-tagged in the machine's grammar — a new knot where the old one hung, tied the week after the year was read aloud. Nobody here believes in accidents. Entered as received; the register does not answer messages, it holds them until they mean something on their own. |
| **The hold mark** | The tag's reading (Page 003, at the price of the keeper's second line): the machine's grammar for a count paused by its own office — not broken, not lost: withheld, deliberately, reversibly, by whoever holds the pen. The colder finding: tied by a hand that does not match the machine's current hand. The thin week was a shield — paper paused cannot be read — using the counter's own public-reading rule as cover. Somebody inside the machine is protecting the basin from being read by slowing the paper down. |
| **For the season** | The machine's explanation, one page too late (Page 003): the permit-ledger discontinued, lamp-oil folded into the fuel-yards' general schedule — the basin's light now counted as part of the basin's fuel, two public schedules merged by a rule nobody living wrote. Machines do not apologize; they reorganize. The season of what, the notice does not say. |
| **The sheltered paper** | The hold, understood from the counter's side (Page 004): the machine's weekly rolls are its own counts, and the basin's terms make every delivery public — so a hold on the delivery is a hold on the MACHINE'S paper being read at the counter of record. Whatever the Office is doing this winter, its own rolls would expose it, and somebody inside has paused them. The register enters the shape and leaves the contents shut. |
| **The shape of the silence** | The register's discipline for a thing it cannot see (Page 004): enter the SIZE of a silence in the machine's own units rather than guessing its contents — one paused count, three weeks of fuel dated on the notice, one ledger nobody is carrying. The notice says *for the season*; the notice's own date gives the season a length, because machines do not guess. |
| **The window keeps its hours** | Kessa's winter ruling (Page 004), spoken from the sill so the queue hears it: terms stand, readings stand, afternoons stand — the basin deliberately stays readable to an unknown protector. A closed counter would hand the holder exactly what a hold is for; the counter's answer to an anonymous gift is to keep the door it came through unlocked. Trust as tactics, entered like a debt. |
| **Cheaper than a closed door** | The risk, priced out loud (Page 004): every reading the basin performs teaches the machine the basin's grammar, and a winter of open hours could have the machine reading the basin back by spring. Kessa pays it anyway — because the alternative hands the protector the silence he is protecting them from. The till's arithmetic, not mercy. |
| **The public hold** | The dusk maintenance at the hatch board (Page 004): one of the board's cords re-tied in machine grammar with two knots changed, the season notice read back knot by knot by a snow-heavy figure with no face. Not a message — a maintenance, done at the hour the basin reads, in the basin's sight. He is telling the counter he will keep his hold where it can be seen, and asking without asking that it be left standing. |
| **"Let him"** | The chapter's ladder run and the page's smallest decision (Page 004): no one sent for, no cord cut, no name entered — the public hold stands, and the register will read it every morning it is there. The basin now has a policy toward its protector that the protector will never learn, which is exactly why it can be trusted. |
| **A break is a fact; a hold is a decision** | The school's winter lesson, said at the benches (Page 005): a broken cord ends and nothing meant it, while *this* cord ends and a hand chose where. The Office's grammar does both, and only one of them has a person in it — which is why the basin reads the FINISH and not a knot further. The machine's coldest instrument, taught as literacy. |
| **The open lesson** | The old school's first class taught outside the Office's walls (Page 005): nine women at benches with practice cords, the counting-lesson readers become students, Kessa charging one line a head on the school's book because a free school is a captured school. A grammar taught outside the Office is the first grammar the Office does not own. |
| **The courtesy form** | The Office's answer to the open window (Page 005): a blank form with a machine seal offering to regularize the counter's hours *for the winter*, in the Office's own schedule. Delivered by the runner, now a junior clerk, with both hands and one pace of distance. Its trap is on the second page. |
| **The second page** | Read aloud at the counter because everything delivered there is (Page 005): the Office may amend the hours by notice, at its ordinary discretion. Written into their schedule, the open window becomes their window with the basin's name on the frame — open by the basin's word, shut by the machine's. |
| **The same courtesy, both ways** | The counter's answer to the courtesy form (Page 005), in the register's third grammar: entered as received — no signature, no line of the basin's on their page — and answered by symmetry: the Office may post its hours on the basin's board, in knot-work, to be read aloud at the counter. To know when the basin is open is to be known when the Office is. |
| **"Note the absence"** | The page's ladder run and its hook (Page 005): at dusk nobody stands at the hatch board, the cords are exactly as the basin left them, and the morning's prints are the only ones. Three explanations — the hold is finished, something inside the walls has changed, or he has been seen — entered without preference, because the register's discipline is to hold a gap open rather than fill it. |
| **The hour that is not on the roll** | The gap found at the hatch board (Page 006): the Office complied with the counter's symmetry by posting its hours in knot-work, six shifts in public — and a seventh group at the cord's end, tied in the wrong hand's finish: a night shift at the fuel-yards that appears on no roll the basin has ever read, and that the Office's own schedule does not carry elsewhere. Whatever is being done inside the machine this winter is being done in an hour the machine does not count. Entered as a gap, never explained. |
| **Posted while the basin slept** | How the Office's hours arrived (Page 006): no clerk, no seal-cart, no boots in the snow, the cord hung overnight. The compliance is real and the manner of it is the confession — a machine that will do the public thing only when the public is asleep. The counter's answer is to read it where it hangs. |
| **The first public purse** | The till's lid opened for the first time in the chapter (Page 006): nine lesson-lines counted down in one entering, the school's book laid between the till and the queue so the book shows one hand and not nine pockets. The second Office now pays for a school that teaches the machine's grammar — the counter's first public money, founded in front of witnesses. |
| **"A school the till owns"** | The price of the purse, said by Kessa while the lid was still up (Page 006): a school paid by the counter is a school the counter can be accused of owning, and the accusation will be made, louder, in the spring. The register enters the risk in both halves rather than hide it — because a school nobody pays for is a school that closes. |
| **Testing the fold** | Rekhak's wordless visit at dusk (Page 006): two fingers on the register's back pocket, eyes on the fold's edge, chain still, in front of a whole square. He does not present the paper, take it back, ask what is on it, or say a word — he confirms that the register keeps its word. The register's entry records only what happened: the fold was tested, and it held. |
| **"Still folded"** | The page's ladder run and its last line (Page 006): the only honest entry a register can make about a paper it has promised not to read. Protection of record, tested by its owner and answered by a fold. |
| **The night watch** | The counter's answer to an hour it may not enter (Page 007): because the Office posted a night shift it does not count and the basin promised not to follow, the window keeps the dark hours instead — one lamp, one reader, three watches of two hours, the shutter up, and the Office's own posted hours read aloud twice an hour to an empty street. A watch made of readers, not of authority: the second Office acquiring a night shift it was never designed to have. |
| **"What midnight costs"** | Kessa's ledger for the watch (Page 007): one measure of oil a night, three watches of two hours, chalked at the till and shown in the morning. A policy with no ledger is a mood — if the square wants a window open at midnight, the square can see what midnight costs. The page's proof that the counter pays for its own principles. |
| **Read to nobody, twice an hour** | The watch's discipline (Page 007): the reader alone in the lamplight reads the Office's hours aloud to a street with nobody in it, all night, starting again from the top. Nobody hears it, which is exactly what makes it law rather than performance — and the first entry Ira records of faith rather than labor. |
| **"Not yet; keep reading; do not follow the hour"** | The sill-knot's whole content, read aloud by the school's own reader (Page 007): the protector's first message in the grammar the basin taught itself on Page 005 — three clauses, wrong-hand finish, no second knot. He knows the basin is watched; he is asking for patience, for the reading to continue, and for nobody to follow the uncounted shift. Entered without inference. |
| **The night the policy cost** | The refusal at the stall's door (Page 007): the figure at the sill within an arm's length, the queue's young watch moving to follow, Kessa's arm across the opening and the slate in her other hand. The counter said it would leave him be; this is the night that promise was expensive, and it held. Entered as a fact and a difficulty — *it was harder than it looks on paper.* |
| **"Answer in kind"** | Dawn on the sill (Page 007), and the page's ladder run: one knot of the basin's own plain tying set where his had lain, meaning the counter's only word — *read.* The first exchange of a correspondence that goes both ways, with a hand the register cannot name, in a grammar both sides learned from the machine. A register that answers has begun something a register cannot end. |
| **The demand of record** | The counter's first legal instrument aimed up-terrace (Page 008): four knots in the machine's own grammar — and only the one word of it that means *this must be answered* — asking the Office to state WHO keeps the uncounted night hour. Not what is done there; who is there. Sealed with the keeper's knot in wax, read aloud at the window before it left. A demand written in the language of the thing addressed cannot be pretended unread. |
| **"Four knots, then the question"** | Nandi's craft rule for the demand (Page 008): their grammar has a word for *this must be answered* and she uses nothing else, because a longer demand could be refused for its manners. The shortest paper is the hardest to call rude. |
| **Nothing goes up unread** | The counter's law turned on its own correspondence (Page 008): whatever the second Office sends up the Council Stair is read aloud to the queue first, so the basin knows what is being asked in its name. If it is answered, the answer belongs to the basin too. |
| **"They read it twice, then told me to walk"** | The runner's account of delivery (Page 008): the Office read the demand twice before answering — the page's only evidence that the question landed — and then sent a man back down a long stair with a slip. The runner takes a pause he was not told to take: *it is not an answer. I am sorry.* |
| **The second disavowal** | The Office's reply to the demand (Page 008): machine seal, one knot-sign, and the formula first used to erase the clerk — *the act of an individual, without authority; not on any roll.* The machine disowns its own posted hour in public, in writing, in front of the basin. It is the cheapest answer the Office has and the most useful paper it has ever given away. |
| **"Whoever keeps it"** | The page's ladder run and the register's blind protection (Page 008): protection of record extended to whoever keeps the uncounted night hour — in the clerk's own form, with the space for a name left empty, entered and NOT read aloud. The first protection the second Office ever granted to a person it has never met; a disavowal names what the machine will not protect, and a thing unprotected can be protected. |
| **The threshold** | Chapter 014's ONE chain-stop, spent at the stall's door (Page 009): the snow-heavy figure admitted into the counter's public room with his hood up and no words at all — the Office's disowned hour entering the second Office's lamplight on a protection the register wrote blind (*whoever keeps it*). Not a blow and not a bargain: an admission, entered by the register as *present.* The second crossing of this arc to be a chain-stop, and the first that is not the enemy's. |
| **"He did not knock"** | The dusk arrival (Page 009): the figure stands in the half-open door with snow on his shoulders and no hand raised — a man who has spent the winter unseen does not know which of the counter's people a knock would be for. The door is opened from the inside, by the keeper, in front of the square. |
| **The protection coming due** | The decision at the counter (Page 009): protection of record was entered for *whoever keeps the night hour* with the name-space empty; a man then arrives to use it. The reader's line is the page's rule — *the paper says whoever keeps it; it does not say whoever we like* — and the bar is lifted because a protection not honoured on the night it is used is a sentence the counter wrote about itself. |
| **The folded sheet** | What the winter's hold was for (Page 009): one machine sheet, blank face, one small knot at its head, its fold worn soft and furred at the crease from months under a coat — set on the counter by the hand that carried it and entered AS RECEIVED, unread, unopened, held. Not the Office's paper any more: the Office disowned the hour that made it. |
| **"Two ways this goes wrong"** | Kessa's pricing of the custody (Page 009), both halves entered in the till's book: the machine learns the counter holds the sheet and demands it lawfully, with a seal, on a case the counter would lose — or the machine never learns it exists, because nobody up-terrace knows, and the hand that carried it is alone with what it did. She refuses to say which is worse; the book says both. |
| **"Read it at dawn"** | The page's ladder run and the counter's custody terms (Page 009): the sheet stays folded for one night under a cord laid in a ring and left untied, then is opened at the window, at dawn, and read aloud with the whole square hearing it at the same hour the keeper does. Public reading as protection both ways — a counter that protects a paper by hiding it has become the thing it fought. |
