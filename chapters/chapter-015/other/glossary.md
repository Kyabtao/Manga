# Chapter 015 — Glossary

New terms and usages introduced in this chapter. Chapter 014's terms are defined in
`chapter-014/other/glossary.md`.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **The thaw** | The opening of Chapter 015 (Page 001): the square in meltwater, eaves dripping, a slab of snow sliding off the stall's awning, the snow gone grey and trodden — and the queue the same length it was in the worst week of winter. Nobody celebrates it: the proof of the second Office is not a crowd that came but a queue that kept coming. |
| **The closed column** | The till's book at the season's turn (Page 001): the fuel column ruled down and crossed at its foot with one chalk stroke — four hundred and one measures, one a night, none of them bought — the oil column closed behind it, and the school's column opened with nine lines in, four paid back, five carried. The first column in the second Office's history that is not a debt somebody owes the counter. |
| **The spring term** | The school's first posted curriculum (Page 001): three lessons a week, the second hour after noon, one line a head carried and not demanded, open to the quarter, hung in knot-work where the Office's hours hung all winter and read aloud to the crowd for correction. The school does not ask leave to teach; it asks the quarter to hold the count. |
| **"What else does the counter buy?"** | The first public accusation of the spring (Page 001), spoken to the keeper's face by a claimant owed seven measures: nine lines went out of the till to buy lessons, the till is not the keeper's, its money is the quarter's, so who decided? The register's answer is to concede the fact before answering — *yes, it did* — and then to put the book in the street. |
| **The purse read aloud** | The counter's answer to an accusation about money (Page 001): the till's book propped on the sill and read to the queue by the woman who wrote it — fuel given not bought, oil spent on the watch, nine school lines out, four back, five carried, no loans taken, and the counter's only takings a cord and two knots. The answer to *who decided* is a column and a window. |
| **The charter** | The Office's first move of the new season (Page 001, dusk): a sealed notice in machine knot-style at the hatch board offering to RECEIVE the school — under the Office, at the Office's cost, with a seal on its lessons and a line in its budget, every lesson counted and every count theirs. The machine's answer to being read back at is not denial but adoption: it offers to own the thing it cannot accuse. |
| **"Choose the owner"** | The page's ladder run and the chapter's opening question (Page 001): the counter will read the charter aloud in the morning, including the seal, and the quarter will have to decide what the school is — the counter's, the Office's, or the street's. A machine that has spent its dodge has found a new grammar, and the answer to it is not a keeper's but a quarter's. |
| **The charter, read in full** | The Office's best paper, taken down off the hatch board and read at the window (Page 002), clause by clause, because nothing is answered where it was hung: six clauses — receive the school, pay every line, seal every lesson, license every teacher, file the term in Office grammar, and take the school's book as a roll of students by name. Five gifts and a list. Read flatly, the empty clause last and twice. |
| **"Five gifts and a list"** | The reader's description of the charter's shape (Page 002), said before the crowd could be won by the gifts. The Office's offer is genuinely generous — sealed lessons cannot be taken away for being the basin's — and the whole of its cost is concentrated in a schedule of names with nothing on it yet. |
| **A door so good saying no is unnatural** | Kessa's naming of the courtesy's mechanism (Page 002): the Office gives the quarter a door and a bill for the hinges, and makes the door good enough that refusing it becomes the strange act. Said quietly, after a mother has stepped forward to enrol her daughter — which is the page's proof that the mechanism works. |
| **The split** | The school's answer to the charter (Page 002), given by Nandi and not by the keeper: the Office may own the GRAMMAR — its lessons, seals, money, licensed teachers and Office-taught hours, at the Office's cost, for every child in the quarter — and the COUNT stays the street's: the school's own book, old grammar, no officer, unsealed and unlicensed, owed nothing by anyone at its table. Two books at one table, each under the other's grammar. |
| **Two books at one table** | The Chapter 013 formula, re-used by a school (Page 002): the machine keeps its seal and its money, the school keeps its count, and neither side loses the thing it actually wanted. The basin's standard for a real ruling — both sides call it a victory and both are right. |
| **The first schedule** | The charter's one clause that cannot be split (Page 002): a roll of students by name. The school's register still holds protection of record with its name-space deliberately empty — *whoever keeps the night hour* — and signing the schedule would write a name the school does not have into an Office hand, while keeping the blank stops the charter being witnessed. Both documents want the same empty space; the page does not fill it. |
| **"Write the name"** | The page's ladder run and its closing problem (Page 002): the school's answer is accepted in principle and blocked in practice by one line. Nobody proposes a false name, nobody guesses, and the page ends in the school's silence with Nandi's two-sentence statement of the wheel. |
| **The licence** | The Office's reply to a partial acceptance (Page 003): five clauses withdrawn overnight and one left on the board — permission to teach the Office's grammar with Office seals and licensed teachers, and no money at all, "because an unfiled roll is an uncounted school, and the Office does not fund what it cannot count." One leaf, one seal, and no cord: the missing budget-cord is the document's loudest feature. |
| **"The cord was gone"** | The first thing the quarter noticed (Page 003): four days of generosity came off the hatch board in the night and left one lease-sized leaf behind, with a clean edge where the charter's budget-cord had hung. A gift withdrawn is cheaper than a request refused, and everyone in the street can see it in the paper's shape before a word is read. |
| **The carrying column** | The school's account after the licence (Page 003): the till reopens the school column as carrying, not funded — benches, slates, oil and the noon bread, eleven lines carried and no new money coming. Kessa pays what the till has, says plainly that it is not enough for a term of children, and asks the quarter to carry the rest. |
| **Nine women, eleven children, no wages** | The school's term under the licence (Page 003): benches carried out crooked on wet flags, a plank across two barrels, cords and chalk-slates, the licence nailed to the wall beside them, Nandi teaching with her lens put away and her hands empty. Unpaid teaching entered for the first time in the register's history as a thing the basin chose rather than a thing done to it. |
| **"No count in any other grammar"** | The licence's condition, found under the seal in border-fine knots (Page 003): a licensed teacher may teach the Office's grammar and may not keep a count in any other grammar. Not about money and never was — the licence is a permission to teach and a prohibition on counting, and holding it means giving up the school's own book. Kessa's line: *it was never about the money.* |
| **"Unlawful, never uncounted"** | Nandi's leaning at the end of the page (Page 003), spoken with her hands flat between the licence and the school's book: hold the licence and stop being a school that keeps a count; keep the count and every licensed teacher teaches unlawfully by the second week. The old school has been unlawful before; it has never once been uncounted. The school has not decided, and the page does not decide for it. |
| **"Teach it anyway"** | The page's ladder run (Page 003): the answer the school gives to a licence with the money taken out — teach the term as posted, in the street, for nothing, with the licence nailed to the wall, and leave the question of the book open until the school itself rules on it. |
| **The purchase declined** | The school's ruling on the licence (Page 004), given by Nandi from the table's head with no keeper in the chair: a permission that forbids counting is not a permission but a purchase, priced at the one thing the school has never sold. The grammar is accepted; the book is not sold; the licence is answered and given back. |
| **Returned read** | How the licence goes back to the Office (Page 004): read, answered in the margin in four knots and the school's plain cord — *the grammar, yes; the count, no* — and nailed up on the hatch board beside the spring term so the whole quarter can read it without help. The first time the street reads an Office paper in Office grammar and gets the joke. |
| **Nine leaves, one accepted** | The knife in the licence's wording (Page 004): the Office licensed TEACHERS, not the school — nine leaves, nine names, one at a time — so that each woman could choose on her own account. One of the school's own counting-lesson women accepts, and is right to: lawful, paid teaching after a winter of teaching for nothing, and a sister to feed. The split arrives from inside the benches. |
| **"Counted, not expelled"** | The school's rule for the split (Page 004): Nandi writes the licensed woman into the school's own book — the book the licence forbids — as a member in good standing, in the old grammar, with the Office's leaf laid on the table beside the page; and the entry is read aloud so nobody can say it was done quietly. Membership depends on where you sit, not on what you carry. |
| **The wage from the Office** | Kessa's accounting of the split (Page 004): the licensed woman is paid by the Office, not by the till, and the school column still says carrying — which means the till has nothing to forgive her for. The page turns a question of loyalty into a column of arithmetic, deliberately. |
| **Two lessons on one step** | The first sealed lesson (Page 004, dusk): the licensed woman teaching the Office's grammar to eleven children on the school's own step with the seal nailed to the wall behind her, while two paces away the unsealed, unlicensed women teach the old grammar from the same benches; the children move between the groups because children do not know they are supposed to choose. The school's book, open in the window behind them, is the page's only comment. |
| **"Count her too"** | The page's ladder run (Page 004): the register's answer to a school that now contains its own licensed teacher — enter her, count her, read it out loud, and let the book prove that the licence failed at the one thing it was written to do. |
| **"Paid, no claim"** | The Office's first money into the basin (Page 005): one coin and a receipt slip reading *paid, no claim*, delivered by a hooded errand-boy at the end of a licensed lesson, in front of the children. Money with no debt against it — which is why Kessa fears it more than an invoice: it does not buy a lesson, it buys the habit of the Office setting money on the quarter's step and being thanked for it. |
| **The coin on the counter** | Where the first Office coin sits from Page 005: not in the drawer, not in anyone's hand, ruled on the slate as *not ours* by Kessa until the school says what it is. The till's way of refusing without an argument. |
| **The roll-slate** | The narrow ruled board given with the purse (Page 005): eleven bare tally lines, no names, to be filled in the licensed woman's own hand and handed to the Office — the condition of the seal. A sealed lesson cannot be withdrawn; an unfiled one can. |
| **"A seal cannot be withdrawn"** | The Office's real lever in the second school (Page 005), spoken by the licensed teacher to the parents: the seal is not a wage, it is the promise, and the promise is genuine — a sealed lesson cannot be taken back by the Office, by a clerk, or by a hand writing a new clause at night. The price is a roll of names. |
| **Nine sealed, two not** | How the parents answered (Page 005): nine children's names written into the Office's roll, two families refusing courteously and keeping their children in the class all the same. The class survives the split intact — the school's rule from Page 004, applied from the parents' side, at a smaller scale. |
| **"One name we still do not have"** | Ira's closing of the day (Page 005): the Office now holds eleven names out of the quarter, and the counter still cannot fill the one line on the school's old page that has been empty for two seasons. The register's copy is larger than the machine's — nine sealed, two unnamed, eleven counted. |
| **"Enter the eleven"** | The page's ladder run (Page 005): the register's answer to the Office's roll — copy it, line by line, into the school's own book, mark the two the Office does not have, and count the class rather than the paper. |
| **"Spent on children not on your roll"** | Kessa's ruling on the Office's coin (Page 006), written on the market receipt that goes back to the Office with the slip: two slates, a twist of chalk and bread for eleven, bought openly at a chandler's stall at the first hour, with not a penny kept. The Office's first money in the basin buys chalk for the two children it will not name, and no clause anywhere can reach the purchase. |
| **The two slates** | The slates bought with the Office's coin (Page 006) and put into the hands of the two children the roll does not hold — the father's son and the grandmother's granddaughter — beside nine children with older chipped slates. The page's argument made in chalk and wood: the machine's money, spent on the children it cannot count. |
| **The wage bowl** | A cracked, wire-mended clay bowl on the school's table (Page 006), started by the two families who refused the roll and filled by the benches teaching without one: a handful of petties for the two lessons a day the Office will not pay for. The quarter's wage-system for what the machine cannot count. |
| **"I am not paying for the seal, I am paying for the child"** | The grandmother's line over the bowl (Page 006) and the chapter's thesis in one sentence: the refusal of the name and the payment of the wage are separate acts, and the school can accept the second without conceding the first. |
| **Two wages, two hands** | The clean arithmetic of the split (Page 006): the Office pays for the nine it counts, the quarter pays for the two it cannot, and Kessa enters both in one column that balances for the first time in nineteen years with the Office in it. Her instruction is unsentimental: *put the bowl money in your sleeve and the Office money in your shoe, and teach eleven.* Not fair — clear. |
| **The audit notice** | The Office's answer to the boycott (Page 006, nailed at dawn beside the licence): the Office will attend the licensed class to verify the roll against the children present, and any child present who is not on the roll will be recorded as taught unlawfully. A procedure rather than a punishment, wholly within the machine's rights, and aimed at the two children holding new slates. |
| **"Pay for the two"** | The page's ladder run (Page 006): the quarter's answer to a class about to be counted — pay the wage for the two the Office refuses, in petties, from a bowl, and keep them in the room while the roll is verified over their heads. |
